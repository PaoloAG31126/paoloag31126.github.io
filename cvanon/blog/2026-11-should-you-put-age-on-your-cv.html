Should you put your age or date of birth on your CV in 2026?

Vetro · 11 November 2026 · ~5 min read

This is one of the most repeated questions in job forums, and the answers usually swing between two extremes: "it's illegal, never do it" and "some jobs require it, so yes." The honest answer sits in the middle, and it is simpler than either of those two positions.

The short answer

No — for the vast majority of roles, your age and your date of birth do not belong on your CV. They rarely add anything a reader needs, and they add something they can use against you. Leaving them out is a reasonable privacy default, and in some countries it is close to the standard practice for anonymous applications. There are a few narrow exceptions, and those are the only ones worth the extra thought.

What a date of birth actually does on your CV

A date of birth is not one piece of information. It is a bundle. From it, a human reader can work out your exact age, and from that, a rough picture of your generation, your school years, your country (the format of the date is a giveaway), and in some cases a gendered or regional pattern in the name. Every one of those is a shortcut the reader can take, fairly or not.

Recruiters will tell you they "do not look at age." In a pile of 200 CVs, in a screening of a few seconds each, that is a statement of intent, not a description of what happens. The less of that bundle you hand over, the less there is to misread.

"I don't hide it so much as I don't put it on there in the first place. The years of experience say it for me, and that is what the role is about."

Why recruiters do not need it

Think about what a recruiter actually uses a CV for. They are checking whether you have done the kind of work the role needs, for long enough, with the right tools. That information is already there, in your work history: the job titles, the companies, the years.

A line like "born 1990" does not strengthen that case. If you have twelve years of experience, the dates in your history already show it. Adding a birth date does not make the experience more real — it only makes you more identifiable.

People who sit on the other side of the table put it plainly: it is not a standard, and it is not an expectation. One of the most upvoted answers to exactly this question, from someone working in HR, was essentially "do not disclose your age; that is not a standard or an expectation, and you have years of experience that already speak for you."

When it might feel necessary

There are a few situations where an age or a date of birth is genuinely relevant, and pretending otherwise would be unhelpful:

  • Roles with a legal minimum age. Some hospitality, retail, and bar roles involve serving alcohol or working late, where the employer is legally expected to confirm you are of age. This is about compliance, not about who you are.
  • Regulated or licensed positions. A few licensed or statutory roles ask for a date of birth as part of a fixed form. If the form has the field, the field is part of the process.
  • Age-graded schemes. Apprenticeships, graduate programs, and some public-service grades are defined by age or by graduation year.

The useful pattern here is timing. If a qualification is legal and needs to be confirmed, it is usually better to confirm it when it matters — in the application form, at the offer stage, or in the first conversation — rather than broadcasting it on page one of a CV that may be read by many people, some of whom will never speak to you. A minimal statement ("eligible to work and serve in the region") is often enough to get to the next step.

What to remove instead

Age and date of birth are the most visible item, but they are not the only one. If you want a CV that is hard to over-identify, these are the lines worth a second look before you send it:

  • Email address — many providers make the name (and sometimes the region) obvious. Use a neutral or the portal's contact address.
  • Phone number — the portal keeps your real number; you do not need it on the page.
  • City / country / district — a strong signal of where you come from and whether you are local. A working arrangement ("Remote (Europe)") says more than a street does.
  • Age and date of birth — explicit ("born 1990") or implicit ("a 34-year-old engineer").
  • Photo — where the culture allows, it is the single strongest age and gender signal on the page.
  • LinkedIn, GitHub, personal site — every one of them is findable, so every one of them undoes the anonymity you are going for.

Bottom line

For almost every role, leave your age and your date of birth off the CV. They do not make the case stronger, and they make you more identifiable to everyone who touches the file. Where a real, legal requirement exists, meet it where and when it matters — not on page one of a document that will travel further than the job.
