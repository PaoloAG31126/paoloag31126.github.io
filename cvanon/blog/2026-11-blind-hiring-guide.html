Blind hiring in 2026 — what to remove from a CV before you apply

Vetro · 7 November 2026 · ~5 min read

More and more employers — public bodies, universities, and a growing number of tech companies — ask applicants to send a CV with the identifying details removed. The idea is simple: judge the work, not the name. It is sometimes called blind hiring, anonymized recruitment or, in the public sector, anonymous application.

The practical question is rarely should I do it? — more often it is what exactly do I remove, and how, without wasting an hour in find-and-replace? This guide walks through it, and at the end links a free, in-browser tool that does most of the work for you.

Why "blind" helps (and where it doesn't)

A CV is a small dossier. It carries the name, the school, the city, the years, the hobbies — and each of those is a shortcut a human reader can (unfairly) take.

Removing them doesn't make hiring perfectly fair. It just removes the easiest biases, the ones that fire in the first three seconds. That is worth a lot in a pile of 200 applications.

"Anonymization isn't about hiding who you are. It's about making sure the first thing a reader sees is what you did, not where you went to school."

The list: what to remove

Here is the checklist most recruiters actually expect to see gone:

  • Name. The first line. Often the most bias-triggering line. Some employers accept initials or a candidate code.
  • Email address. Many email providers make the name and sometimes the region obvious (maria.rossi@…). Use the application portal's contact address instead.
  • Phone number. Same reason. The portal keeps your real number.
  • City / country / district. The biggest giveaway of background and of whether you are local. Replace with the working arrangement: Remote (Europe), On-site (region, flexible).
  • LinkedIn, GitHub, personal site. All of them are findable, so they all break the anonymity. Remove or move them to a "details" sheet you send later if asked.
  • Age and date of birth. Either explicit ("born 1988") or implicit ("12-year-old developer").
  • Family situation. Marital status, children, nationality — where they appear, out they go. (In some countries, this is also a legal question: see below.)
  • School, university. Where the rule is strict. Where it is not, keep it — it is a legitimate part of a CV.

The list: what to keep

Removing too much makes the CV look evasive. Keep:

  • The actual skills, projects, tools and years of professional experience.
  • Where the employer's rule allows: the school, the languages (a language list is a normal part of a CV, not personal data), the professional licenses and certificates.
  • A working email address — just not a name-revealing one.

Legal background (EU)

In the EU, the GDPR says a recruiter should only ask for the personal data they actually need to run the process. In practice that means the CV you hand over at the application stage should be the minimum viable version. The employer can ask for your identity later — when it matters. Some public-sector rules go further: in Italy, France, and parts of the Netherlands, anonymized application is a requirement, not an option.

This guide is not legal advice. If the posting you are applying to is silent, the safest path is to ask the recruiter: "Should I send an anonymized version of my CV?" One line of email, and the answer is unambiguous.

How to anonymize a CV in practice

There are three ways, in order of friction:

  1. By hand. Open the CV, delete the obvious items, save a copy. Reliable, slow, and easy to miss the implicit bits ("born 1988", "based in Lyon").
  2. Find-and-replace, semi-automated. Build a list of your own identifiers and search them. Better. Still manual, and you are the one who forgets something.
  3. A purpose-built tool. Paste the text, press a button, get the result. CVAnon is one of these: it runs 100% in your browser, never uploads the file, and removes the common identifier patterns (email, phone, social links, age phrases, common city names) in one pass.

A short, honest review of tools

There are two flavours of "anonymize a CV" tool on the web:

  • Server-side tools. The CV is uploaded to a service, the service strips identifiers, and you download the result. Convenient, and fine for most people. But the file leaves your machine, and the service has to promise (and deliver) that it doesn't keep it.
  • Client-side tools. The anonymization happens on your machine, in the browser. Nothing leaves. You can verify this yourself — open your network tab and watch. CVAnon works this way.

For most people either is fine. If your CV contains anything you would not want to leave your machine — a medical note, a home address, a family situation — the client-side version is the safer default.

Workflow I actually use

  1. Paste the text of the CV into CVAnon.
  2. Read the output top to bottom. Where something that should stay was removed, fix it. Where something that should go is still there, delete it manually.
  3. Paste the result back into your template. Reformat.
  4. Do one final search on the original for the strings you know you own: your name, your handle, your city, your email domain.
  5. Save a separate file named something like cv-blind.pdf for these applications, so your normal CV is untouched.

Final notes

Blind hiring is not a magic fix. It does reduce the most obvious biases, and that is enough to matter. If the application you are writing requires it, you owe it to yourself (and to the other candidates) to take the extra minute and do it properly.

If it doesn't require it, doing so is still a reasonable privacy default: the less personal data you put into an application, the less of it can be leaked, reused, or used to identify you.

That is the whole argument. Everything else is logistics.
